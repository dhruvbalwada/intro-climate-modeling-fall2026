What this course is

Last year, GR5001 taught you what the climate system does — and how to interpret climate information.

This course opens the machine that produces that information:

  • Build the model hierarchy yourselves (in Python)
  • Understand how the real Earth System Models work
  • Analyze their output the way practicing scientists do (CMIP6)
  • Judge what models can and cannot tell you

The one-sentence identity: “you know what the climate does; now, how we know what it will do.” Callbacks to GR5001 will happen every single week — most concepts get rebuilt, properly, with math and code. A year of decay is assumed and fine (Mingfang agrees).

The ladder we’ll climb

Every rung still in active use. The ladder is the course map — today we climb it historically.

Key framing: not a ladder of progress where the top makes the bottom obsolete — a ladder where every rung has a permanent job. Today: meet each rung through the people and problems that created it.

Rung 1: The budget

Energy in = energy out

1896: Arrhenius computes the climate by hand

  • Motivated by ice ages, not fossil fuels
  • Months of hand calculation: radiative budget + water-vapor amplification
  • His answer for CO₂ doubling: ~5–6 °C (modern: 2.5–4 °C)
  • The structure of the calculation is recognizably modern

Story beats: Langley’s infrared measurements as data; Arrhenius working through a difficult personal period; got the structure right, the number high. In 1908 he thought warming would take millennia and might be nice — why nobody panicked. 1938 Callendar: one sentence — linked rising CO₂ to observed warming from weather stations, was politely dismissed.

What Arrhenius’s model was

\[\text{absorbed sunlight} = \text{emitted infrared}\]

One equation. One perturbation (CO₂). One answer (a temperature).

Weeks 2–3: you build exactly this — then make it tick in time, grow an ice cap, and misbehave.

Point at the ladder: rung 1 never retired. FaIR-type models running IPCC scenario numbers today are direct descendants. (Don’t say FaIR yet — “the models the IPCC uses to score policy scenarios.”)

Rung 2: The forecast

Equations marched forward in time

1922: Richardson’s magnificent failure

  • Hand-integrated the fluid equations for a 6-hour forecast over Europe
  • Result: pressure change of ~145 hPa — absurdly, spectacularly wrong
  • But the method — grid + finite differences + timestepping — is exactly what every model does today

Why it failed: unfiltered gravity-wave noise in the initial conditions (don’t dwell — “his equations were too honest; they amplified noisy data”). The forecast-factory image: 64,000 human computers with a conductor — it’s literally a parallel supercomputer, 80 years early. Callback in week 5.

Weather vs climate — the distinction that saves us

Weather forecast = initial-value problem — where does this trajectory go? Chaos (Lorenz) kills it beyond ~2 weeks.

Climate projection = boundary-value problem — how do the statistics shift when you push the system?

Casino analogy: no one can predict the next hand. The house margin is a certainty.

THE misconception-killer of week 1 — students will hear “chaos means you can’t predict 2100” forever. GR5001 gave them weather-vs-climate qualitatively; this is the mathematical version. Lorenz: predictability of the first vs second kind.

1950: ENIAC, and a lesson in modeling

Charney, Fjørtoft & von Neumann: the first numerical weather forecast.

Their winning move: simplify — filter Richardson’s equations down to one level of quasi-geostrophic flow.

Progress came from a smarter simple model, not a bigger computer.

The course’s thesis in one anecdote. The 24-hour forecast took ~24 hours of ENIAC time — “keeping pace with the weather.” This moral repeats: Stommel (wk 4), the EBMs, FaIR.

Rung 3: The column

One vertical slice of atmosphere, done right

1967: Manabe & Wetherald

  • 1-D radiative-convective column: real spectroscopy + convection pinning the lapse rate
  • CO₂ doubling → ECS ≈ 2.3 °C — within today’s assessed range
  • 2015 poll of climate scientists: the most influential climate-change paper ever

Suki Manabe: arrived from Tokyo 1958, GFDL. The insight: get one column right before doing the sphere. Fig. 16’s quiet bombshell — stratospheric cooling while the surface warms: a fingerprint prediction, made decades before it was observed, that pure solar warming would NOT produce. Week 2 builds the toy version; week 9 returns for feedbacks.

Rung 4: The sphere

General circulation models

1956: Phillips spins up a planet

Two-level model, one hemisphere, a month of simulated time.

Jets and storm tracks appeared — nobody coded them.

This is emergence: put conservation laws on a grid, and the general circulation is what falling into balance looks like.

Phillips’s numerical experiment ran on a machine with ~5KB of memory. Emergence is week 5’s big idea — plant the phrase “you never coded a Hadley cell” now.

The lineage to today

  • 1969 Manabe & Bryan: first coupled atmosphere–ocean model
  • 1975 Manabe & Wetherald: first 3-D CO₂-doubling experiment → polar amplification predicted
  • 1979 Charney Report: with two GCMs in the world, ECS = 1.5–4.5 °C
  • 2021 AR6: ECS = 2.5–4 °C

42 years to narrow the bar by half. Why so slow? (Hold that thought until Nov 5.)

Plant the week-9 mystery explicitly — it’s the best cliffhanger the field has. Polar amplification: predicted in ’75, now observed at ~4x global rate in the Arctic. Predictions-that-came-true list is the honest counterweight to the tuning question.

Rung 5: The enterprise

From models to a global scientific infrastructure

Modeling becomes coordinated science

  • 1988 Hansen testifies to the Senate; 1990 first IPCC report
  • 1995→ CMIP: every modeling center runs the same experiments — models become comparable, and their spread becomes data
  • Carbon, chemistry, vegetation, ice sheets join → GCMs become Earth System Models
  • 2021 Nobel Prize in Physics: Manabe & Hasselmann

CMIP is Act IV’s subject: the students will use CMIP6 data themselves from HW1 onward (lightly) and deeply from week 11. The Nobel framing: “physics recognized that climate physics is physics.”

The frontier (where this course ends)

  • Km-scale simulation: resolving storms and ocean eddies globally — computationally heroic
  • AI emulators: neural networks forecasting weather competitively, now creeping into climate
  • The perpetually unsolved: clouds

Why keep the whole ladder? (Held, 2005)

Climate science needs a hierarchy of models the way biology needs model organisms.

  • Simulation ≠ understanding — you can simulate what you don’t understand
  • A gap between them is dangerous: you can’t tell trustworthy projections from artifacts
  • Simple models are where understanding lives — and they discover things (a 1961 two-box model found a possible AMOC collapse before any GCM could)

Held’s essay is the intellectual backbone of the course design — say so. The Stommel teaser sets up week 4. If anyone wants one reading from today: this one, it’s short and equation-free.

Fit for purpose

“Which model is best?” is meaningless without “…for what question?”

Question Right tool
Why is there a greenhouse effect? pencil + 0-D EBM
Will this El Niño bring drought? initialized coupled GCM
Warming in 2100 under scenario X? ESM ensemble + simple-model emulator
Could the AMOC collapse? box model (mechanism) + ESM (when/whether)

Note the last row deliberately has TWO answers — mechanism rung + projection rung. That’s the segue to the activity, where several questions do too.
