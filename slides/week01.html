A system in my office

Before any climate: let’s model something together.

The rules of the game

  • I have a system: water flows in (with some “weather” in it), the level responds
  • I’ve measured inflow and level for a long time — you get the data
  • I will not tell you what’s inside
  • Your job: a simpler description good enough to predict situations we haven’t seen

That’s modeling. That’s also this entire course, in one sitting.

What just happened (part 1)

  1. A model = a simpler description of a complicated system, built for a purpose
  2. Statistical models are real models — but they interpolate; change the system and they fail (week 13: remember this about AI)
  3. Budgets — in − out = accumulation — are where physical modeling starts
  4. Equilibrium budgets already predict a lot (and found the nonlinearity the line couldn’t)

What just happened (part 2)

  1. Time dependence = lags + adjustment, governed by a stabilizing feedback (fuller → drains harder)
  2. Even physical models are calibrated — we fitted \(k\); we lived with the evaporation bias (week 10 is entirely about this)
  3. Every model has a regime of validity — discovered, usually, the hard way
  4. Different questions → different models: average level vs. flood timing. “Best” depends on the question

One more thing — the thing

For the Earth, there is no box to open.

How we got here — the hierarchy, historically

Everyone you’re about to meet was climbing the same ladder we just climbed.

1896: Arrhenius computes the climate by hand

  • Motivated by ice ages, not fossil fuels
  • Months of hand calculation: a radiative budget + water-vapor amplification
  • His CO₂-doubling answer: ~5–6 °C (modern: 2.5–4 °C) — high, but the structure is modern
  • He was doing exactly our Model 1: an equilibrium budget

~50 min total for the history segment — it’s tightened; keep moving. Color: 1908 he guessed warming would take millennia and be pleasant → why nobody panicked. Callendar 1938 (station data, dismissed) in one breath. Tease: “we’ll do Arrhenius’s first step ourselves before you leave.”

1922: Richardson’s magnificent failure

  • Hand-integrated the fluid equations for a 6-hour forecast: absurdly wrong
  • But grid + finite differences + timestepping = exactly what every model does today
  • His failure: too-honest equations amplified noisy data

The forecast factory is a parallel supercomputer, 80 years early (week 5 callback). Bridge to next slide: the fix wasn’t a bigger machine.

1950: ENIAC — a smarter simple model wins

Charney, Fjørtoft & von Neumann: first numerical forecast — by simplifying (one quasi-geostrophic level filtered out Richardson’s noise).

Weather vs climate: forecast = initial-value problem (chaos kills it in ~2 weeks); climate = boundary-value problem (statistics under a push).

The casino: no one predicts the next hand. The house margin is a certainty.

Two beats on one slide to save time: the thesis anecdote + THE misconception-killer. If pressed on “but chaos!”: forced response vs internal variability — the bucket’s level statistics under doubled inflow were predictable even though its wiggles weren’t.

1956–1975: columns, spheres, coupling

  • Phillips 1956: two-level hemisphere → jets emerged, uncoded — put conservation on a grid and circulation is what balance looks like
  • Manabe & Wetherald 1967: the 1-D column done right — ECS ≈ 2.3 °C; predicted stratospheric cooling (observed decades later); voted most influential climate paper ever
  • Manabe & Bryan 1969: ocean coupled · M&W 1975: first 3-D CO₂ doubling → polar amplification predicted

Compressed from three slides. Manabe from Tokyo to GFDL 1958; “get one column right before the sphere.” Fingerprint predictions made before observations = honest counterweight to the tuning worry.

1979 → 2021: the error bar and the enterprise

  • Charney Report 1979 (two GCMs on Earth): ECS = 1.5–4.5 °C → AR6 2021: 2.5–4 °C. 42 years to halve the bar — why so slow? (Nov 5.)
  • CMIP (1995→): every center runs the same experiments — model spread becomes data
  • GCMs absorb carbon, chemistry, ice → Earth System Models
  • 2021: Nobel Prize to Manabe & Hasselmann

Plant the week-9 mystery explicitly. CMIP = Act IV’s subject; students touch CMIP6 data from HW1.

Now for real

Earth is a bucket.

The mapping

bucket Earth
tap (inflow F) sunlight
drain infrared radiation to space
water level h temperature T
fuller → drains harder warmer → radiates harder (the stabilizing feedback)
level lags the tap warming lags emissions (week 3: the ocean sets the clock)

The payoff slide of the day — the demo wasn’t an analogy, it was the actual mathematical object of this course. ~15 min for this closing science segment.

The first calculation (live, on the board)

Energy in: sunlight on a disk — \(\pi R^2 S_0(1-\alpha)\). Energy out: infrared from a sphere — \(4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4\).

\[T_e = \left[\frac{S_0(1-\alpha)}{4\sigma}\right]^{1/4} \approx \mathbf{255\ K} = -18\,°\mathrm{C}\;\;(?!)\]

formula actual
Mars ~210 K ~218 K ✓
Earth 255 K 288 K — off by 33 K
Venus ~230 K 735 K — off by 500 K!

Board derivation, slowly — ask “why πR² in but 4πR² out?” before answering; sets the math culture. This is Arrhenius’s step 1, and our Model-1 equilibrium budget for the planet. Venus kills “it’s distance from the Sun.” In Lab 0 this week they plot exactly this formula.

33 K — the most productive wrong answer in science

That gap is the greenhouse effect. Closing it:

  • Week 2: why — the layer model + the picture professionals use
  • Week 3: when — the model ticks in time; the ocean sets the clock
  • Week 9: how much — feedbacks decide whether 3.7 W/m² means 2 °C or 5 °C

Honest note: 255 K is REAL — satellites measure Earth radiating at exactly this temperature. The puzzle is precise. Cliffhanger, then logistics.
