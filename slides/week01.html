1 · What is climate?

Weather is the state. Climate is the distribution.

  • Weather: the value right now — a random variable, unpredictable in detail beyond ~2 weeks
  • Climate: the distribution those values are drawn from — mean, variability, tails — estimated over a chosen period (convention: ~30 years)
  • Climate change = the distribution shifting, widening, or reshaping — with very different consequences in the tails

~5 min. Plant it abstractly here; both demos make it concrete (bucket histogram, Central Park histogram). Subtlety worth a sentence: climate itself must be estimated from a finite, possibly drifting record — the rarest extremes may not even be in your window.

2 · What is a model?

A system in my office

Before any climate: let’s model something together.

The rules of the game

  • I have a system: water flows in (with some “weather” in it), the level responds
  • I’ve measured inflow and level for a long time — you get the data
  • I will not tell you what’s inside
  • Your job: a simpler description good enough to predict situations we haven’t seen

That’s modeling. That’s also this entire course, in one sitting.

Before the models: pause on the raw data plot and name the concepts (next slide).

The bucket has weather — and a climate

  • The level wiggles constantly (noisy tap) — that’s its weather
  • But its statistics are steady: mean ≈ 1, a typical spread, a typical excursion time — that’s its climate

Climate = the statistics of a system. You’ve now defined it — on a bucket. Earth’s version: means, variability, extremes over ~30 years.

What just happened (part 1)

  1. A model = a simpler description of a complicated system, built for a purpose
  2. Statistical models are real models — but they interpolate; change the system and they fail (week 13: remember this about AI)
  3. Budgets — in − out = accumulation — are where physical modeling starts
  4. Equilibrium budgets already predict a lot (and found the nonlinearity the line couldn’t)

What just happened (part 2)

  1. Time dependence = lags + adjustment, governed by a stabilizing feedback (fuller → drains harder)
  2. Even physical models are calibrated — we fitted \(k\); we lived with the evaporation bias (week 10 is entirely about this)
  3. Every model has a regime of validity — discovered, usually, the hard way
  4. Different questions → different models: average level vs. flood timing. “Best” depends on the question

One more thing — the thing

Today, when our models failed, we opened the box and checked.

For the Earth, there is no box to open.

No spare planet, no truth-generator to read. Everything climate science knows about 2100 comes from models earning trust the way ours did today: predicting things they weren’t fitted to.

Let this land in silence for a beat. It’s the epistemological heart of the course — and quietly, the honest frame for the ‘aren’t models just tuned?’ question, which gets its full session in week 10. Optional aside: climate scientists simulate today’s luxury deliberately — “perfect-model experiments,” using one GCM as fake truth to test methods.

3 · What is uncertainty?

Three reasons a forecast can be wrong

“It’s tough to make predictions, especially about the future.”

  • Model uncertainty — wrong structure (missing overflow spout) or wrong parameters (mis-fitted k)
  • Initial-condition uncertainty — chaos: the wiggles can’t be tracked beyond a horizon (why CMIP6 ignored the actual weather)
  • Scenario uncertainty — we don’t know what anyone will pour: the inflow — or the emissions — is a human choice, not physics

Different ones dominate at different time horizons. (Week 11 makes this quantitative.)

~5 min. Every item points back at something they just watched: the spout, the r=0.00, the doubled tap. For Earth by 2100, scenario uncertainty is often the biggest — a strangely hopeful fact: the largest unknown is a choice.

One thermometer, three models

The bucket had a knowable truth. Now the real world.

Central Park, daily temperature, 1995–2014

Three versions of the same place:

  1. The station — a real thermometer (NOAA GHCN)
  2. ERA5 reanalysis — a weather model corrected by observations every few hours
  3. A CMIP6 climate model — free-running: it makes its own weather

Commit before we look: which of 2 and 3 will match the station day by day? Both? Neither? One?

Switch to station_vs_models.ipynb and live-drive: obs + histogram → seasonal-fit statistical model (82% of variance — the most predictable thing in climate) → the reveal: ERA5 rides the black curve (r=0.94), CMIP6 ignores it (r=0.00) → fair comparison = statistics vs statistics → the three mean temperatures (13.1 / 11.5 / 10.2 °C): representativeness + bias. ~18 min in notebook.

What that just taught us

  • Know what kind of model you’re looking at: statistical fit · observation-corrected reanalysis · free-running model — each trustworthy for different things
  • A free-running model matching weather day-by-day would be suspicious; matching the distribution is its actual job
  • Differences ≠ failure: grid cell vs. park (representativeness), and genuine bias — diagnosed, published, corrected (→ weeks 10, 13)
  • The 5-parameter seasonal fit beats the CMIP6 model at describing today — and is mute about 2100. Fit for purpose.

This is the real-world twin of the bucket’s “no box to open”: for Earth we never get the truth generator — models earn trust by their statistics and out-of-sample behavior.

Now for real

Earth is a bucket.

The mapping

bucket Earth
tap (inflow F) sunlight
drain infrared radiation to space
water level h temperature T
fuller → drains harder warmer → radiates harder (the stabilizing feedback)
level lags the tap warming lags emissions (week 3: the ocean sets the clock)

The payoff slide of the day — the demo wasn’t an analogy, it was the actual mathematical object of this course. ~15 min for this closing science segment.

The first calculation (live, on the board)

Energy in: sunlight on a disk — \(\pi R^2 S_0(1-\alpha)\). Energy out: infrared from a sphere — \(4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4\).

\[T_e = \left[\frac{S_0(1-\alpha)}{4\sigma}\right]^{1/4} \approx \mathbf{255\ K} = -18\,°\mathrm{C}\;\;(?!)\]

formula actual
Mars ~210 K ~218 K ✓
Earth 255 K 288 K — off by 33 K
Venus ~230 K 735 K — off by 500 K!

Board derivation, slowly — ask “why πR² in but 4πR² out?” before answering; sets the math culture. This is Arrhenius’s step 1, and our Model-1 equilibrium budget for the planet. Venus kills “it’s distance from the Sun.” In Lab 0 this week they plot exactly this formula.

33 K — the most productive wrong answer in science

That gap is the greenhouse effect. Closing it:

  • Week 2: why — the layer model + the picture professionals use
  • Week 3: when — the model ticks in time; the ocean sets the clock
  • Week 9: how much — feedbacks decide whether 3.7 W/m² means 2 °C or 5 °C

Honest note: 255 K is REAL — satellites measure Earth radiating at exactly this temperature. The puzzle is precise. Cliffhanger, then logistics.
