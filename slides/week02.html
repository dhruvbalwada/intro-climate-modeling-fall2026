Lab 0d · Modeling using budgets

The plan

  1. A budget: \(A\,\dfrac{dh}{dt} = F - Q_\mathrm{out}\)
  2. Two drain laws from two dominant balances: Torricelli \(Q \propto \sqrt{h}\) · Poiseuille \(Q \propto h\) — we adopt the linear one, \(Q = kh\)
  3. Equilibrium: \(h^* = F/k\) — the level the bucket wants
  4. Stepping in time: Euler, \(h_{n+1} = h_n + \Delta t\,(F - k h_n)/A\)
  5. Play: vary \(h_0\), \(k\), \(F\) — keep the plots, they come back in ten minutes

The notebook drives; the board carries the derivations (they are written out in Lab 0d for reading later). Step 5 is what the uncertainty slides point back at.

3 · What is uncertainty?

Three reasons a forecast model can be wrong

“It’s tough to make predictions, especially about the future.”

You just generated all three in the bucket:

  • Initial-condition uncertainty — you varied \(h_0\): matters early, forgotten later (Earth: today’s exact weather)
  • Model uncertainty — you varied \(k\): parameters (and structure) (Earth: we don’t know exact laws or our models can’t be exactly solved)
  • Scenario uncertainty — you varied \(F\): the pourer decides, not physics (Earth: emissions)

Different ones dominate at different horizons.

Every bullet points at something they DID today. Worth saying aloud: by 2100 the biggest uncertainty is the scenario — the largest unknown is a choice.

How the three trade off with time

Individual model runs under a low and a high scenario. For the next ~20 years the wiggles — internal variability — swamp the difference between scenarios.
Share of the uncertainty in global, annual-mean, 10-year-averaged temperature: internal variability dominates early, model uncertainty mid-century, scenario by 2100. That smoothing is why orange fades so fast — ask about one summer in one city and it stays orange much longer.

4 · Now for real

Earth is a bucket.

The mapping

bucket Earth
tap (inflow \(F\)) sunlight
drain infrared radiation to space
water level \(h\) temperature \(T\)
drain law \(Q = kh\) \(\sigma T^4\) (Stefan–Boltzmann)
fuller → drains harder warmer → radiates harder (the stabilizing feedback)
level lags the tap warming lags emissions (soon: the ocean sets the clock)

The payoff slide of the day — the bucket you just built wasn’t an analogy, it was the actual mathematical object of this course. Ask them to fill the right column before revealing it.

The first calculation (live, on the board)

Energy in: sunlight on a disk — \(\pi R^2 S_0(1-\alpha)\). Energy out: infrared from a sphere — \(4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4\).

\[T_e = \left[\frac{S_0(1-\alpha)}{4\sigma}\right]^{1/4} \approx \mathbf{255\ K} = -18\,°\mathrm{C}\;\;(?!)\]

with \(S_0 = 1361\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\), \(\alpha \approx 0.30\), \(\sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}\)

formula actual
Mars ~210 K ~218 K ✓
Earth 255 K 288 K — off by 33 K
Venus ~230 K 735 K — off by 500 K!

Board derivation, slowly — ask “why πR² in but 4πR² out?” before answering; sets the math culture. This is the bucket’s equilibrium step (set the budget to zero) for the planet. Venus kills “it’s distance from the Sun.”

33 K — the most productive wrong answer in science

That gap is the greenhouse effect. Closing it:

  • Next: why — the layer model, then the picture professionals use
  • Then: when — the model ticks in time; the ocean sets the clock
  • Later: how much — feedbacks decide whether 3.7 W/m² means 2 °C or 5 °C

Honest note: 255 K is REAL — satellites measure Earth radiating at exactly this temperature. The puzzle is precise. If time remains, go straight into “why”.

If we get there · Closing the 33 K gap

Step 1: Why the atmosphere intervenes

Two streams of light that barely touch

  • Sun (5800 K): peaks near 0.5 µm — shortwave
  • Earth (255–288 K): peaks near 10–15 µm — longwave
  • Almost no overlap → energy in and energy out are separate bookkeeping

This spectral separation is a gift — it’s why “solar in, infrared out” is a clean split and why the whole subject is tractable. GR5001 callback: they saw the Trenberth-style budget diagram; this is the physics under its two-column layout.

Who absorbs what

  • N₂ + O₂ = ~99% of the air — and nearly transparent in the infrared
  • The absorbers are the vibrating, polar trace molecules: H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, O₃, N₂O
  • Nearly transparent at 0.5 µm, strongly absorbing at 10–15 µm

One sentence of molecular physics is enough: symmetric diatomic molecules have no dipole moment to grab infrared photons; triatomic benders and stretchers do. Don’t go deeper unless asked.

The greenhouse effect is a trace-gas effect

“CO₂ is only 0.04% of the atmosphere!”

Correct — and that 0.04% (plus water vapor) is doing all of the work. Remove every trace gas and keep the 99%: Earth freezes.

Reframe the talking point as the actual story. Plant (don’t fully develop) the week-9 distinction: H₂O is the biggest absorber, but it condenses — days of residence, slaved to temperature — so it’s a feedback, an amplifier. CO₂ is the forcing, the control knob. Lacis et al. 2010 ran it: remove CO₂, the water vapor rains out, Earth ices over. Full treatment Nov 5.

Step 2: One pane of glass

The simplest greenhouse: setup

One atmospheric layer that is:

  • transparent to shortwave (sunlight passes through)
  • perfectly absorbing in longwave (nothing gets past it)
  • radiating \(\sigma T_a^4\) both up and down

BOARD, not slides, for the whole derivation. Two budgets, two unknowns — ask the room to supply each term before writing it. This is the second board derivation of the course (after week 1’s T_e); same culture: they follow, not admire.

The derivation (record)

Top of atmosphere: what leaves = what enters \[\sigma T_a^4 = \tfrac{S_0}{4}(1-\alpha) \;\Rightarrow\; T_a = T_e = 255\ \mathrm{K}\]

Surface: heated by Sun and by the sky \[\sigma T_s^4 = \tfrac{S_0}{4}(1-\alpha) + \sigma T_a^4 \;\Rightarrow\; T_s = 2^{1/4}\,T_e \approx 303\ \mathrm{K}\]

Pause on what fell out: the ATMOSPHERE sits at the emission temperature; the surface is warmer than both. The layer doesn’t “trap” heat — it re-radiates downward. Kill the blanket metaphor here (blankets suppress convection; Wood’s 1909 rock-salt greenhouse experiment if asked).

Three surprises from one tiny model

  1. The atmosphere radiates at 255 K — the surface hides beneath it, warmer
  2. The surface receives more energy from the sky than from the Sun (~340 vs ~168 W/m² in the real budget)
  3. It overshoots: 303 K predicted vs 288 K observed

#2 is the intuition-breaker — let it sit. It’s in the observed surface budget (GR5001’s diagram!): downwelling longwave beats absorbed solar at the ground. #3 is the productive failure that drives the next slide.

Why the overshoot? The best kind of wrong

  • The real atmosphere isn’t a perfect absorber (windows, wings)
  • Deeper: convection short-circuits radiation — ~100 W/m² leaves the surface as evaporation + thermals, not photons
  • Pure radiative equilibrium would make the lower atmosphere absurdly steep and unstable → convection overturns it and pins the lapse rate ≈ 6.5 K/km

Automating exactly this repair = Manabe & Wetherald 1967 (week 1’s “most influential paper”).

This is the week’s brief vertical-structure aside — the ONLY dose of RCE this week (thread returns Nov 5). GR5001 callback: their week 3 was convection; “you know why air rises — here’s what that does to the greenhouse.” M&W Fig 16 from last week can reappear for one beat.

Step 3: The picture professionals actually use

Where does Earth’s radiation to space really come from?

In the CO₂/H₂O bands the atmosphere is optically thick: surface photons are absorbed and re-emitted many times before escaping.

Effectively, space “sees” radiation not from the ground but from an effective emission height:

\[z_e \approx 5\text{–}6\ \mathrm{km}, \quad \text{where } T \approx 255\ \mathrm{K}\]

Connect back: the 255 K from week 1 is REAL — satellites measure it. This slide says where it lives: ~5.5 km up. The layer model was a cartoon of this.

Add CO₂ → the radiating surface rises

  1. More CO₂ → atmosphere more opaque in the IR → z_e rises
  2. Higher = colder (lapse rate!) → planet radiates less to space
  3. Energy imbalance → the whole column warms…
  4. …until the new emission height is back at 255 K
  5. The surface, ~6 km below along the lapse rate, ends up warmer

\[\Delta T_s \approx \Gamma \cdot \Delta z_e \qquad (\sim150\ \mathrm{m\ rise} \approx 1\ \mathrm{K})\]

Walk the 5 steps slowly, twice if needed — this is the mental model to install for the whole course. Note what restores balance: not the surface “trapping” anything, but the planet’s radiating level sliding up a fixed temperature ladder.

One sentence to keep

Global warming = raising the height from which Earth radiates to space — with the surface dragged warmer along the lapse rate.

Say it, let them write it, move on. This sentence defeats arguments the layer model can’t (next block).

This picture makes free predictions

  • The stratosphere should cool as the surface warms (it sits above the emission level) — observed ✓, and a fingerprint pure solar warming would not produce
  • No lapse rate → no warming from raising \(z_e\) → the lapse rate itself matters (week 9: lapse-rate feedback)

Stratospheric cooling: predicted by M&W 1967 (they saw it in Fig 16 last week), confirmed decades later by satellites/radiosondes. This is the honest counterweight to “models are tuned” — fingerprint predictions made before the observations existed.

Step 4: The 125-year-old objection

1900: Ångström vs Arrhenius

“The CO₂ absorption band is already saturated — the center of the 15 µm band absorbs everything within meters. Adding more CO₂ can’t do anything.” — Knut Ångström’s lab assistant, 1900 (paraphrased)

This argument killed interest in Arrhenius’s work for decades. It still circulates today.

By the end of this hour, you’ll be able to break it. (That’s literally the next activity.)

Frame it with respect — it’s a GOOD objection, and the physics community bought it for ~40 years. Don’t reveal the answer yet.

Look at the actual spectrum

  • Band center: opaque at 280 ppm, opaque at 560 ppm — Ångström was right about the center
  • Band wings: visibly deepening — this is where doubling acts

LIVE DEMO if wifi cooperates (U. Chicago MODTRAN): set 280 ppm, note outgoing flux; double to 560; the flux drops ~3 W/m²; zoom the band wings. Students see the forcing in measured-quantity space. Screenshots on this slide are the wifi-failure fallback — rehearse the demo once beforehand.

Why the response is a logarithm (not zero)

Each doubling widens the effective opaque interval by roughly the same spectral increment:

\[\Delta F \approx 5.35 \cdot \ln\!\left(\frac{C}{C_0}\right)\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}} \qquad \approx 3.7\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\ per\ doubling}\]

  • Saturation is why it’s a log — not why it’s zero
  • This is why climate speaks in doublings, and why ECS is defined per doubling

280→560 ppm and 560→1120 ppm have the SAME forcing. Also note: this one-line formula is how CMIP-model forcings are quoted and compared — the toy is the ruler (bridge beat returns at the end).

Your turn: break Ångström (in pairs)

Here is the argument, verbatim:

“The CO₂ band is already saturated — the atmosphere absorbs all the radiation in that band within a few hundred meters. More CO₂ cannot change the energy balance.”

With your partner, write the strongest 3–4 sentence rebuttal you can. Use anything from today. Names on the slip; hand it in.

Circulate with the TAs. Most pairs will find the wings; the deeper rebuttal (emission height rises regardless — a saturated band just means z_e is HIGH, not that it can’t go higher) is what the debrief adds. Slips → participation credit.

Debrief: three independent ways it fails

  1. The wings aren’t saturated — and that’s where doubling acts (you saw it in the spectrum)
  2. The emission height rises regardless — saturation means \(z_e\) is high, not that it’s stuck; higher is colder is less radiation
  3. Thin, cold upper layers always radiate less — there is no altitude where “more absorber” stops mattering

Argument 2 is the professional one — flag that pairs who found it were reasoning at the research level. Historical coda, 1 sentence: the 1940s–50s spectroscopy (partly military-funded, Plass et al.) resolved the band structure and revived CO₂ science.

From today’s toy to the real machine

Scale-up: radiation in a real climate model

  • Line-by-line absorption: physically exact, computationally hopeless at runtime
  • Models use band schemes (“correlated-k”), validated against line-by-line codes
  • Radiation is computed in every column, ~100 levels, every 1–3 model hours

Your layer model and a GCM’s radiation code differ in resolution, not in kind.

The point of the scale-up beat every week: demystify. The radiation module of a CMIP model is the N-layer model with real spectroscopy and clever bookkeeping.

Bridge: the toy is the ruler

  • Backward: Manabe & Wetherald 1967 = today’s chain done right the first time — real spectra + convection-pinned lapse rate → ECS ≈ 2.3 K, within today’s assessed range
  • Forward: when CMIP models are compared, their CO₂ forcing is quoted through the same \(5.35\ln(C/C_0)\) — the formula you now own

Close the loop explicitly — this is the weekly bridge sentence: “the simple model isn’t a stepping stone we discard; it’s the instrument the community measures the big models with.” Next week’s bridge is even stronger (their lab EBM is literally fit to every CMIP model).
