3 · What is uncertainty?

Three reasons a forecast model can be wrong

“It’s tough to make predictions, especially about the future.”

You just generated all three in the bucket:

  • Initial-condition uncertainty — you varied \(h_0\): matters early, forgotten later (Earth: today’s exact weather)
  • Model uncertainty — you varied \(k\): parameters (and structure) (Earth: we don’t know exact laws or our models can’t be exactly solved)
  • Scenario uncertainty — you varied \(F\): the pourer decides, not physics (Earth: emissions)

Different ones dominate at different horizons.

Every bullet points at something they DID today. Worth saying aloud: by 2100 the biggest uncertainty is the scenario — the largest unknown is a choice.

Individual model runs under a low and a high scenario. For the next ~20 years the wiggles — internal variability — swamp the difference between scenarios.

From a water budget to an energy budget

Inflow minus outflow changes what a reservoir stores.

The bucket stores water

\[\frac{dV}{dt}=F_{\mathrm{in}}-F_{\mathrm{out}}\]

\(V\): water volume, \(\mathrm{m^3}\)
\(F\): water flow rate, \(\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}\)

The planet stores energy

\[\frac{dU}{dt}=P_{\mathrm{absorbed}}-P_{\mathrm{emitted}}\]

\(U\): internal energy, \(\mathrm{J}\)
\(P\): power, \(\mathrm{W=J\,s^{-1}}\)

For the planet, divide by its fixed surface area \(A_p=4\pi R^2\):

\[E=\frac{U}{A_p}\quad[\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}}],\qquad Q=\frac{P}{A_p}\quad[\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}].\]

\(E\) is stored energy per area. \(Q\) is a global-mean energy flux.

Start with the bucket equation students already used. A stored amount and a rate have different units. Here U denotes internal energy relative to a fixed reference; in this model we track its temperature-related changes. P_absorbed already subtracts reflected sunlight. Dividing by area changes how we report the budget, not the conservation law. Q here means radiative energy flux, not the water drainage rate called Q in Lab 0d. The mean includes the night side. No assumption of equilibrium is required.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, section “From a water budget to an energy budget”.

Energy storage and temperature

The stored amount determines the state we observe.

Bucket: volume and water level

\[\Delta V=A_b\,\Delta h\]

\[\frac{dV}{dt}=A_b\frac{dh}{dt}\]

\(A_b\): bucket cross-sectional area, \(\mathrm{m^2}\)
\(h\): water level, \(\mathrm{m}\)

Planet: energy and temperature

\[\Delta E=C\,\Delta T\]

\[\frac{dE}{dt}=C\frac{dT}{dt}\]

\(C\): heat capacity per area, \(\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}\)
\(T\): temperature, \(\mathrm{K}\)

Ordinary heat capacity \(C_{\mathrm{total}}\) has units \(\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\). Here we use \(C=C_{\mathrm{total}}/A_p\), because our energy budget is per unit area.

We assume constant \(C\), no phase changes, and negligible mechanical work.

For the same added energy per area, does a larger \(C\) give more or less warming?

Answer: less warming, since Delta T = Delta E / C. Use a wider bucket as the parallel: the same added water gives a smaller rise. We conserve energy, while temperature describes the reservoir’s state. Changes in internal energy matter here, so we do not need an absolute zero for U or E. Ordinary heat capacity C_total is the energy needed to warm the whole modeled reservoir by 1 K, in J/K. For a mass m with specific heat capacity c, C_total = mc, where c has units J/kg/K. We divide C_total by the same planetary area used to define E. Our C therefore has units J/m^2/K and must not be divided by area again. Its numerical value and the effective amount of material taking part in the heating remain unspecified. We are not assuming the entire solid planet warms uniformly. Defer C = rho c H until it helps answer a question.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “Connecting energy storage to temperature”.

Sunlight averaged over the planet

\(S_0\) is solar power per unit area perpendicular to the rays.

Near Earth’s orbit: \(S_0\approx1361\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\).

Which area? Size Role in the budget
The planet’s shadow: a disk \(\pi R^2\) Intercepts sunlight
The whole surface: a sphere \(4\pi R^2\) Defines our global average

\[P_{\mathrm{incident}}=S_0\pi R^2\]

\[Q_{\mathrm{incident}}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{incident}}}{4\pi R^2} =\frac{S_0\pi R^2}{4\pi R^2} =\frac{S_0}{4}\approx340\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\]

This average includes the night side. Individual locations receive different amounts.

Imagine a screen behind the planet, perpendicular to parallel sunlight. Its circular shadow tells us how much of the beam the planet intercepts. The day side is a hemisphere, but tilted patches intercept less energy per unit surface area than patches facing the Sun. Counting the shadow accounts for that tilt. We then divide the intercepted power by the entire planetary area to define a global mean. The factor four does not require rotation or uniform temperature. It is not a measurement of local sunlight everywhere. 1361 is an approximate measured solar irradiance near Earth’s orbit; 340 follows from dividing it by four. Hold S0 fixed in this model. We have not subtracted reflected sunlight yet.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “A disk intercepts; a sphere provides the averaging area”.

Albedo and absorbed sunlight

Albedo \(\alpha\) is the fraction of incoming sunlight reflected to space.

\[Q_{\mathrm{reflected}}=\alpha\frac{S_0}{4}\]

\[Q_{\mathrm{absorbed}}=Q_{\mathrm{incident}}-Q_{\mathrm{reflected}} =(1-\alpha)\frac{S_0}{4}\]

For \(S_0=1361\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\) and \(\alpha=0.30\):

\[Q_{\mathrm{absorbed}}=0.70\times\frac{1361}{4} \approx238\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\]

We borrow \(\alpha=0.30\) from present-day Earth, including its clouds and atmosphere. This is a reference choice for our bare-rock model.

If albedo increases while sunlight stays fixed, does the planet absorb more energy or less?

Answer: less, because a larger fraction is reflected. Alpha is dimensionless, between zero and one. Present-day Earth’s reflected fraction is approximately 0.30; it combines reflection by clouds, the atmosphere, and the surface. An actual atmosphere-free planet need not have this albedo. With the unrounded numbers, incident flux is 340.25 W/m^2, reflected flux 102.075, and absorbed flux 238.175. Approximate values are sufficient here. Reflected sunlight never enters our thermal energy reservoir, so the storage equation uses absorbed sunlight rather than total incident sunlight. For fixed S0 and alpha, this term acts like the bucket’s imposed constant inflow. No equilibrium assumption yet.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “Reflection reduces the absorbed fraction”.

Thermal emission from the surface

The surface loses stored energy by emitting radiation, even at night.

For a blackbody surface, the Stefan–Boltzmann law gives

\[Q_{\mathrm{emitted}}=\sigma T^4\]

\(T\) must be in kelvin. \(\sigma=5.67\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}\).

We treat Earth’s surface as a perfect blackbody at thermal wavelengths, with one temperature. The law already gives flux per unit emitting area, so there is no extra factor of four.

Our energy budget now has both radiation terms:

\[C\frac{dT}{dt}=\frac{(1-\alpha)S_0}{4}-\sigma T^4.\]

With sunlight and albedo fixed, a warmer surface loses energy faster.

Thermal radiation carries energy away from the material and depends on its temperature. Reflected sunlight redirects incoming solar energy. These are different processes. At Earthlike temperatures, thermal emission is mainly infrared. A surface at zero Celsius still emits, because zero Celsius is about 273 K. Emissivity one is a simplifying assumption for thermal wavelengths, consistent with nonzero solar albedo because the wavelength ranges differ. The total emitted power is 4 pi R^2 sigma T^4, so dividing by surface area gives sigma T^4 directly. For a nonuniform surface, average T^4 would matter, not the fourth power of average T. Our one-temperature model deliberately omits that structure. Connect increasing emission with the bucket’s increasing drainage as water level rises. We have now specified the time-dependent model but have not solved its equilibrium. The next block will set dT/dt to zero and examine the temperature it gives.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “How does the planet lose energy?” and “The time-dependent energy balance model”.

The bare-rock equilibrium

At equilibrium, temperature is steady: \(dT/dt=0\).

\[0=\frac{(1-\alpha)S_0}{4}-\sigma T_{\mathrm{eq}}^4\]

Move emission to the other side, then divide by \(\sigma\):

\[\sigma T_{\mathrm{eq}}^4=\frac{(1-\alpha)S_0}{4}, \qquad T_{\mathrm{eq}}^4=\frac{(1-\alpha)S_0}{4\sigma}.\]

Take the positive fourth root:

\[T_{\mathrm{eq}}=\left[\frac{(1-\alpha)S_0}{4\sigma}\right]^{1/4}.\]

Heat capacity drops out because no energy accumulates. Sunlight and thermal radiation still flow through the system.

Begin with the full differential equation on the preceding slide. We impose equilibrium rather than assuming all situations are in equilibrium. The left side C dT/dt becomes zero for any finite positive C. Equality of the two fluxes gives the first rearrangement. The bracket in the final expression has units K^4. Select the positive root because T is absolute temperature. Keep the assumptions from the earlier slides: prescribed sunlight and albedo, one surface temperature, no atmosphere, and blackbody thermal emission. Increasing C changes how fast temperature responds but not this equilibrium.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “Finding the balance”.

A surface temperature that is too cold

Using \(S_0=1361\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\), \(\alpha=0.30\), and the Stefan–Boltzmann constant:

\[T_{\mathrm{eq}}=\left(\frac{238.2}{5.67\times10^{-8}}\right)^{1/4} \mathrm{K}\approx255\ \mathrm{K}.\]

Temperature in kelvin Temperature in Celsius
Our bare-rock model \(\approx255\ \mathrm{K}\) \(\approx-18\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)
Earth’s mean surface \(\approx288\ \mathrm{K}\) \(\approx15\,^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)

We use Earth’s present-day albedo, even though our model has no atmosphere. A truly airless Earth would have a different albedo.

Could a larger heat capacity close this roughly 33 K gap?

Answer: no. With sunlight, albedo, and emission unchanged, C cannot change the equilibrium. Our unrounded calculation gives about 254.6 K or -18.6 C. The slide uses the conventional rounded reference values 255 K and -18 C; Earth’s 288 K mean is also approximate. For this model at equilibrium, both incoming and outgoing fluxes are about 238 W/m^2. About 255 K is also Earth’s effective emission temperature: the blackbody temperature corresponding to the outgoing thermal flux. It is not an actual temperature at the top of the atmosphere, nor a direct measurement of a uniformly cold Earth. The model identifies the emitting surface with the surface whose temperature we observe; the atmosphere will separate those concepts. The comparison motivates changing the radiation pathways rather than tuning heat capacity.

What changes when we add an atmosphere?

Our model surface is too cold.

So far, we have assumed that all radiation emitted by the surface escapes directly to space.

An atmosphere can absorb and emit radiation, changing that energy flow.

To understand how, first compare the wavelengths of sunlight and Earth’s radiation.

Connect this step to the preceding temperature comparison. Increasing heat capacity cannot change the equilibrium of our bare-rock model. We now add a missing part of the physical system: the atmosphere and its interaction with radiation. Keep the prescribed solar input and albedo for this next model so we can isolate the effect of atmospheric longwave absorption and emission. The following spectrum slide explains why incoming sunlight and outgoing terrestrial radiation can interact differently with the same atmosphere. We will then connect absorption to emission with Kirchhoff’s law, trace the energy flows, and write separate budgets.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “Why the atmosphere intervenes”.

Sunlight versus Earth’s radiation

The atmosphere’s absorption depends on wavelength. It transmits much of the Sun’s shortwave radiation but absorbs strongly in parts of Earth’s longwave infrared.

Both curves describe thermal emission. The hotter Sun emits most strongly at much shorter wavelengths than Earth’s surface. Shortwave includes visible and near-infrared sunlight. Longwave here refers to terrestrial infrared. The graph shows idealized emission per unit wavelength, with a logarithmic wavelength axis. Each curve has its own normalization, so equal peak heights do not mean equal power. These are source spectra, not solar irradiance received at Earth. The wavelength contrast alone does not explain the greenhouse effect: atmospheric absorption also varies with wavelength. Water vapor, carbon dioxide, and clouds absorb terrestrial infrared. Kirchhoff’s law: absorption and emission

At a given wavelength, a good absorber is also a good emitter.

\[\text{absorptivity at }\lambda = \text{emissivity at }\lambda\]

  • Absorptivity: the fraction of incoming radiation a material absorbs.
  • Emissivity: its emission relative to a blackbody at the same temperature and wavelength.

The atmosphere absorbs and emits strongly in some infrared bands, while absorbing weakly at many wavelengths of sunlight.

This law applies under local thermodynamic equilibrium, a good approximation for the lower atmosphere. The amount emitted also depends on temperature.

Our atmospheric budget therefore needs emission as well as absorption.

The equality applies at the same wavelength and for corresponding directions, at the material’s temperature. Both coefficients range from zero to one. A good emitter means efficient relative to a blackbody at its own temperature, not necessarily a large absolute flux. A cold good emitter can radiate less than a warmer weak emitter. Local thermodynamic equilibrium concerns the local molecular populations. It does not require the atmosphere to have the same temperature as the surface, or require either reservoir’s energy budget to be in equilibrium. Do not equate shortwave absorptivity with longwave emissivity. In particular, one minus solar albedo does not give thermal emissivity. Atmospheric thermal emission occurs in all directions, giving both upward and downward fluxes in our layer model. It is distinct from reflection of surface radiation. Kirchhoff’s law alone does not set the atmosphere’s temperature. That comes from its energy budget.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, discussion of Kirchhoff’s law and the gray longwave approximation.

A fully absorbing atmospheric layer

Two reservoirs: surface at \(T_s\) and one atmospheric layer at \(T_a\).

  • The layer transmits all sunlight and absorbs all surface longwave radiation.
  • In the longwave, both surface and layer act as blackbodies. The layer emits upward and downward.

Keep prescribed albedo: \(Q=(1-\alpha)S_0/4\) reaches the surface. Omit convection and evaporation.

Compare with the preceding diagram: the direct surface-to-space arrow has disappeared. This is a deliberately idealized limit, not a claim that the real atmosphere absorbs every infrared wavelength. We retain the net solar input Q after reflection, even though this idealized layer itself transmits all sunlight. The surface absorbs the downward longwave radiation completely. Surface emission is sigma Ts^4. Each face of the layer emits sigma Ta^4. One atmospheric temperature represents a reservoir, not a resolved vertical temperature profile or the actual temperature at the top of the atmosphere. All quantities are fluxes per unit planetary area. The schematic arrows indicate directions, not magnitudes.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, one-layer model assumptions. Figure adapted from atmosphere_energy_flows.svg, with direct longwave transmission removed.

Two reservoirs, two energy budgets

Each budget follows rate of storage = input − output, with \(Q=(1-\alpha)S_0/4\).

Surface

\[C_s\frac{dT_s}{dt}=Q+\sigma T_a^4-\sigma T_s^4\]

Absorbed sunlight + downward atmospheric radiation − surface emission.

Atmosphere

\[C_a\frac{dT_a}{dt}=\sigma T_s^4-2\sigma T_a^4\]

Absorbed surface radiation − upward and downward atmospheric emission.

\(C_s\) and \(C_a\) are constant heat capacities per unit area, in \(\mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}\). We leave their values unspecified for now.

Why does atmospheric emission have a factor of two, but surface emission does not?

Trace each term back to the preceding diagram. The surface absorbs net sunlight and downward atmospheric longwave radiation, and emits upward. The atmosphere absorbs all upward surface longwave radiation and emits from both faces. Each atmospheric emission term is sigma Ta^4, giving two terms in the atmospheric loss. The surface is the lower boundary and radiates upward into the atmosphere only in this model. The factor of two counts two emitting faces. It is unrelated to the factor of four used to average sunlight over a sphere. The left sides are rates of energy storage per unit area, with units W/m^2, matching every right-hand term. Neither reservoir needs to be in equilibrium. Temperatures are in kelvin. We have not yet set the time derivatives to zero or chosen heat capacities. Pause here before combining the equations or solving their equilibrium.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, time-dependent surface and atmospheric energy budgets.

The whole-system energy budget

Add the surface and atmospheric budgets:

\[\begin{aligned} C_s\frac{dT_s}{dt}+C_a\frac{dT_a}{dt} &=(Q+\sigma T_a^4-\sigma T_s^4)\\ &\quad +(\sigma T_s^4-\sigma T_a^4-\sigma T_a^4). \end{aligned}\]

Surface emission and downward atmospheric radiation are internal transfers. Each is a loss from one reservoir and a gain for the other.

\[C_s\frac{dT_s}{dt}+C_a\frac{dT_a}{dt}=Q-\sigma T_a^4\]

Total rate of energy storage = absorbed sunlight − radiation escaping to space.

Does zero total energy imbalance guarantee that both temperatures are steady?

Read the two parentheses as surface input minus output, then atmospheric input minus output. We have expanded the atmospheric loss into upward and downward emission. The negative surface-emission term cancels the atmospheric gain. The positive downward-radiation term cancels one atmospheric loss. The remaining atmospheric loss is emission to space. This is conservation for a boundary enclosing both reservoirs and holds during adjustment as well as at equilibrium. All terms have units W/m^2. No energy is created by the internal exchanges.

Answer to the question: no. One reservoir could gain energy while the other loses an equal amount. Equilibrium of this model requires both dTs/dt and dTa/dt to vanish separately. A zero total imbalance at one instant is insufficient.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “Add the budgets to check conservation”.

Equilibrium of the one-layer model

Set both \(dT_s/dt=0\) and \(dT_a/dt=0\):

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Surface:}\quad Q+\sigma T_a^4&=\sigma T_s^4\\ \text{Atmosphere:}\quad \sigma T_s^4&=2\sigma T_a^4. \end{aligned}\]

Substitute the atmospheric balance into the surface balance:

\[Q+\sigma T_a^4=2\sigma T_a^4,\qquad Q=\sigma T_a^4.\]

With \(Q\approx238\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\):

\[T_a=\left(\frac{Q}{\sigma}\right)^{1/4}\approx255\ \mathrm{K}\]

\[T_s=2^{1/4}T_a\approx303\ \mathrm{K}\]

Heat capacities drop out again. The surface is warmer even though absorbed sunlight is unchanged.

The atmospheric balance gives Ts^4 = 2 Ta^4, hence Ts = 2^(1/4) Ta when taking positive roots in kelvin. Use Q = 238.175 W/m^2 and sigma = 5.67e-8 W/m2/K4 to obtain Ta = 254.58 K and Ts = 302.75 K, conventionally rounded to 255 and 303 K.

Check the fluxes verbally: the surface receives about 238 W/m^2 from sunlight plus 238 from the atmosphere and emits about 476. The atmosphere receives 476 and emits 238 upward plus 238 downward. Only 238 enters and leaves the whole system. The larger surface emission represents internal exchange. It does not imply that the atmosphere creates energy. Downward radiation can flow from a cooler atmosphere to a warmer surface while the net longwave exchange remains upward from the warmer surface.

The atmospheric layer equals the effective emission temperature in this particular model because it supplies all outgoing thermal radiation as a blackbody. This is not a measurement of the real top-of-atmosphere temperature. We still have not specified the heat capacities or solved the transient response.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “Equilibrium as a special case” and “Check the equilibrium fluxes numerically”.

Does the atmosphere have to absorb all the surface’s infrared radiation?

Our next refinement: partial absorption, allowing some surface radiation to escape directly to space.

Other simplifications remain, including one atmospheric temperature and no convection or evaporation. Partial absorption is only one refinement.

The temperature comparison is for the same prescribed sunlight and albedo in both models. Adding a physically meaningful process need not immediately produce the correct numerical answer. We have explained how a surface can be warmer than the effective emission temperature while conserving energy, but have not made a complete model of Earth’s climate.

Real atmospheric absorption varies with wavelength, as discussed earlier. Some surface infrared radiation passes through relatively transparent wavelength ranges. Recall the direct surface-to-space arrow in the general energy-pathways diagram, which disappeared in the fully absorbing limit. We now have a physical reason to restore it. Partial absorption alone is not a complete explanation of the discrepancy. Matching Earth’s mean surface temperature would not validate every assumption or guarantee correct responses to perturbations. In the next block we can introduce a longwave absorption fraction and, consistently with Kirchhoff’s law under a gray approximation, reduce the layer’s emission too. Do not change absorption alone while retaining blackbody atmospheric emission.

Partial infrared absorption

The layer absorbs a fraction \(\epsilon\) of surface infrared radiation. The fraction \(1-\epsilon\) escapes directly to space, with \(0\leq\epsilon\leq1\).

By Kirchhoff’s law, the layer emits \(\epsilon\sigma T_a^4\) in each direction.

We use one \(\epsilon\) across all longwave wavelengths: the gray approximation. We neglect longwave reflection and keep the surface black in the longwave.

Reintroduce the direct surface-to-space path from the earlier qualitative diagram. The two red arrows partition one total surface emission sigma Ts^4. They are not two additional emissions. The atmosphere absorbs epsilon sigma Ts^4, and the remainder passes through without heating the layer. The purple arrows each carry epsilon sigma Ta^4. Kirchhoff’s law applies wavelength by wavelength under local thermodynamic equilibrium. A single common coefficient for these broadband fluxes requires our additional gray assumption, rather than following from Kirchhoff’s law alone. The same epsilon multiplies atmospheric absorption and emission, but the temperatures in the fluxes differ. Sunlight and albedo remain prescribed, the layer remains transparent to sunlight, and we still omit convection and evaporation. Q already accounts for reflected sunlight. Epsilon describes radiative behavior, not a CO2 concentration. Arrow widths are schematic.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “Introduce one absorption parameter” and “Revise the arrows, then the budgets”. Diagram panel reused from Lab 1, figures/one_layer_fluxes.svg.

Budgets with partial absorption

Surface

\[C_s\frac{dT_s}{dt}=Q+\epsilon\sigma T_a^4-\sigma T_s^4\]

The surface still loses its entire emission, wherever that radiation goes.

Atmosphere

\[C_a\frac{dT_a}{dt}=\epsilon\sigma T_s^4-2\epsilon\sigma T_a^4\]

Only the absorbed fraction heats the layer. Emission leaves in both directions.

Adding the budgets gives:

\[C_s\frac{dT_s}{dt}+C_a\frac{dT_a}{dt} =Q-\left[(1-\epsilon)\sigma T_s^4+\epsilon\sigma T_a^4\right].\]

The bracket is outgoing longwave radiation (OLR): direct surface radiation + upward atmospheric emission.

Trace each term on the preceding diagram. A common mistake is to put epsilon in front of the surface’s emitted flux in the surface budget. The surface loses all sigma Ts^4, regardless of whether the atmosphere absorbs it or it reaches space. Another mistake is to count transmitted radiation as heating the layer. The atmosphere’s two loss terms both carry epsilon, consistent with its absorptivity under the gray assumption. Adding the equations cancels downward atmospheric radiation and leaves the unabsorbed part of surface emission plus upward atmospheric emission as the losses to space. This whole-system balance holds throughout adjustment, not just at equilibrium. All terms have units W/m^2. Q=(1-alpha)S0/4. Cs and Ca remain constant heat capacities per unit area.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “Revise the arrows, then the budgets”, and the Lab 1 model equations.

Equilibrium with partial absorption

Set both temperature tendencies to zero. For \(\epsilon>0\), the atmospheric balance gives:

\[0=\epsilon\sigma T_s^4-2\epsilon\sigma T_a^4, \qquad T_a^4=\frac{T_s^4}{2}.\]

Substitute into the surface balance:

\[0=Q+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\sigma T_s^4-\sigma T_s^4, \qquad Q=(1-\epsilon/2)\sigma T_s^4.\]

\[T_s=\left[\frac{Q}{\sigma(1-\epsilon/2)}\right]^{1/4}\]

Absorption Equilibrium surface temperature
\(\epsilon=0\): transparent layer \((Q/\sigma)^{1/4}\approx255\ \mathrm{K}\)
\(\epsilon=1\): fully absorbing layer \((2Q/\sigma)^{1/4}\approx303\ \mathrm{K}\)

At \(\epsilon=0\), solve the surface budget directly. The atmospheric budget becomes \(0=0\) and leaves \(T_a\) undetermined.

In the atmospheric equilibrium equation, divide by epsilon sigma only when epsilon is positive. This gives Ta4=Ts4/2, or Ta=Ts/2^(1/4). Substituting into the surface budget gives the expression displayed. For positive epsilon, the cancellation in the atmospheric equation does not mean atmospheric temperature is independent of epsilon: it follows the changing surface temperature. The surface formula extends continuously to epsilon=0, but at exactly zero the atmosphere neither absorbs nor emits, so it retains its initial temperature in this model. We must not use the divided atmospheric equation to assign its temperature there.

For fixed Q, surface equilibrium temperature increases with epsilon. The endpoints recover our earlier models. Intermediate values can give a temperature near Earth’s roughly 288 K, which students will explore in Lab 1. Matching one observed temperature is calibration, not validation of the whole model. In general Ta is no longer the effective emission temperature because radiation to space comes from both reservoirs. At equilibrium, OLR still equals Q. Heat capacities affect adjustment but not this equilibrium.

Source: course instructor notes, chapter 2, “Solve for equilibrium” and “Check the endpoints and interpret the result”.
