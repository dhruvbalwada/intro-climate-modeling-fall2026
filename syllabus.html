Course Description

Earth system and climate models are critically important tools for climate science research. They are used to study climate variability in the past, determine how climate change has contributed to recent trends and extreme events, and understand how various natural and anthropogenic forcing affect the evolution of the climate system now and into the future. This class serves as an introduction to the history, development, process representations, and practical application of earth system models.

Students will learn the history and evolution of earth system modeling and how these models have been used to inform some of the most important topics in climate science (e.g., detection and attribution, future projections, climate sensitivity). Lectures, and associated lab work, will introduce the processes integrated into various components of earth system models (e.g., atmosphere, ocean, land, carbon cycle, etc), important interactions between these components (e.g., climate system feedbacks, climate sensitivity), and how earth system models are used for future projections. Students will familiarize themselves with the wealth of climate model simulation data available from free public archives (e.g., CMIP6, the Multi-Model Large Ensemble Project), the protocols used for designing and running simulations, and practical tools for analyzing these datasets.

Classroom lectures will be supplemented by practical lab-assignments, where the students will use and develop their own models demonstrating the concepts learned in class. As a final project, students will develop their own research questions using available climate model simulations for their primary analyses.

Prerequisites:

  • CLMT GR5001 Dynamics of Climate Variability and Change

  • CLMT GR5045 Computing and Research Methods for Climate (or proven familiarity with scientific programming)

Course Objectives

  • Develop a foundation in hierarchy of models of climate, used to assess and understand the climate system.

  • Have a basic understanding of modern climate models, used to simulate the past and future climate.

  • Exposure to the Climate Model Intercomparison Project, the associated protocols, and how these datasets are used by the climate enterprise

  • Learn how to interact with these models and datasets and analyze them

  • Complete a project that requires formulating a climate relevant question, and using existing climate model data or data generated from a simple climate model to answer it.

Required and Recommended Readings

There are no required readings.

The course is loosely based on a few books. There will be suggested readings from these texts each week as extra supplemental learning in addition to the classroom teaching:

  • The Climate Modeling Primer, 4th Ed, K. McGuffie & A. Henderson-Sellers

  • Climate Dynamics, 2nd Ed. K. Cook

  • Climate Change & Climate Modeling, J.D. Neelin

  • Demystifying Climate Models: A Users Guide to Earth System Models, A. Gettelman & R. B. Rood (open access)

The students are not expected to purchase any of these. All suggested reading will be provided to the students through Courseworks or via email.

Attendance and Class Participation (5%)

We expect you to attend and actively participate in class. We may incorporate in-class group discussions and activities, which may require you to submit sample problems that you worked out individually or in a group, with help from TA and instructor. This work will be submitted in class on a physical piece of paper; please bring the necessary implements to class. There is no late submission for this work.

Lab sessions (10%)

There will be a Lab session every few weeks, which would involve going over the computational aspects of the concepts that are being discussed in class. During these sessions the students would be required to use their laptops to follow along using the Jupyter notebooks that will be provided by the instructor or TA.

These notebooks will have follow along exercises, which should be completed collaboratively in class with the help of the instructor and TA. The students would be required to submit their work by the end of the day through Github.

Homework Assignments (50%)

Homeworks will be assigned on roughly a bi-weekly basis following material presented in class.

There will be 5 assignments. Similar to the labs, these assignments will usually be in the form of Jupyter notebooks, with problems requiring data analysis, running simple simulations, and interpretation of results that are obtained. The solutions will be submitted through Github.

Details will be provided as needed.

Final project (35% = proposal 10% + project 25%)

As a final project, students will develop their own research questions using either available climate model simulations for their primary analyses, or by generating data using any of the simpler models discussed in class or those relevant to the field. The project should be submitted as a reproducible Jupyter notebook documenting the analysis that was undertaken and the insights that were drawn from the data, model, and analysis.

Examples of past projects and the submission process can be found on https://earth-env-data-science.github.io/projects.html

Topics should be submitted to the instructor by Friday of the 5th week of classes (October 9) for approval, and a short proposal will be due in week 7, Friday October 23 (1 page max). Timely submission of these components will count towards 10% of the grade, and late assignment policy (see below) would apply.

The project proposal must include a short write up of the planned work, annotated reference to any papers that will be used to design the project, and a confirmation that the datasets needed for the project have been identified and checked for accessibility.

The final project will require an in class presentation, which will take place on 10 or 17 December. The students will also be required to submit a final project Jupyter notebook, which will be due on 18 December, and will be evaluated in lieu of a final exam.

Late Assignment Policy

If an assignment is received after the posted deadline, it will be penalized by 10% of the total earned grade for each day late, up to a maximum of one week (7 days).

To accommodate exceptional circumstances (e.g., health issues, family emergencies, or religious holidays), each student is allowed one late homework assignment without penalty (up to one week).

Academic Integrity

The Columbia Climate School holds each member of its community responsible for understanding and abiding by the Academic Integrity and Community Standards. Ignorance of policies and standards concerning academic dishonesty shall not be a defense in any disciplinary proceedings.

Students should be aware that academic dishonesty (for example, plagiarism, cheating on an examination, or dishonesty in dealing with a faculty member or other university official) are particularly serious offenses and will be dealt with severely under Dean’s Discipline. It is essential to the academic integrity and vitality of this community that graduate students do their own work and properly acknowledge the circumstances, ideas, sources, and assistance upon which that work is based. Academic honesty in class assignments and exams is expected of all students at all times.

Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) is also subject to these integrity standards and must never be employed for a use that would be considered plagiarism if that material were sourced from a human author. For example, submitting work under your name that you did not personally write is not allowed, regardless of whether it was written by another author or by an AI tool. Individual faculty may have different policies on other ways students may use or not use AI in a particular class. Students should assume all use of AI is forbidden unless told otherwise.
