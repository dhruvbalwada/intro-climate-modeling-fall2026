Investigating a physical question with environmental data

Working in groups of 3–4, you will investigate a question about the climate or another environmental system using data. You may choose one of the suggested topics below or develop your own project, provided it meets the same basic requirements.

Your data may come from climate model simulations, observations, or another documented environmental source. Data generated through experiments with a simple physical model are also suitable. Reproducing a published result or an IPCC figure is one possible starting point; an IPCC connection is not required.

The most important outcome is your own understanding. By the end of the project, every team member should be able to explain the physical system, justify the analysis, and discuss the nuances and limitations of the conclusions, including when AI has helped with the work.

Teams and dates

With 31 students, we will have nine teams of three and one team of four. Every team member should contribute to the analysis and be able to explain the physical question, the main processing steps, and the final figures.

Milestone Date What to submit or prepare
Project topic and team selection Friday, October 23 Team member names and selected topic, either from the suggestions below or your own idea.
In-class presentations December 10 and 17 A 30-minute slot per team: plan for 20 minutes presenting, 8 minutes of questions, and 2 minutes for transition.
Final project notebook Friday, December 18 Reproducible notebook, supporting files, and presentation slides.

As stated in the syllabus, topic and team selection counts for 10% of the course grade, and the final project counts for 25%. The syllabus late-assignment policy applies. The October 23 milestone is topic and team selection; a written proposal is not required.

Discuss your question, data source, and scope with the instructor or a TA early. Student-designed projects are welcome as long as they meet the requirements below. The suggested topics are starting points, and teams may adapt them to their interests.

Basic project requirements

  1. Use data from a climate model, observations, or another environmental source. Identify where the data come from, what the variables represent, and the spatial and temporal scales they describe. Explain relevant uncertainties and limitations of the source.
  2. Ask a question that requires physical understanding. Your analysis must go beyond reporting means, trends, correlations, or other basic descriptive statistics. Those calculations can be useful steps, but you must connect the results to physical processes, mechanisms, or constraints and use evidence to evaluate an explanation of how the system works.
  3. Explain and check the analysis. Document how you move from the source data to your conclusions. Justify your choices and show checks that could reveal meaningful errors in processing or interpretation. Consider alternative explanations and whether your conclusions depend on a particular method, parameter, or subset of the data.
  4. Demonstrate personal understanding. Every student must be able to explain the question, relevant physics, methods, results, and limitations in their own words, and respond thoughtfully to follow-up questions. This expectation applies regardless of how much AI assistance the team uses.

For example, a plot of ocean temperature over time could become a physical investigation of heat storage and its relationship to the energy budget. A comparison of rainfall trends could investigate the roles of atmospheric moisture, circulation, or energy constraints. The depth comes from the question, reasoning, and interpretation; using a complicated statistical method by itself does not establish physical understanding.

Choose a manageable question and use relevant scientific literature to develop your explanation. Define the quantities, units, and equations you use and explain their assumptions. If you reproduce a published result, compare it carefully with your own and explain discrepancies. If you develop your own analysis, identify the evidence or physical expectations against which you will evaluate it.

Suggested topics

These 12 optional ideas illustrate possible projects. You may choose, adapt, or propose an alternative to them. Reproducing an IPCC figure is optional, and the approaches and figure references below are suggestions rather than prescribed analyses.

Suggested topic Physical question Possible approach Possible IPCC connection
1. Climate sensitivity How can we estimate equilibrium warming before a model equilibrates? CMIP6 energy-budget regression Chapter 7, Box 7.1, Figure 1
2. Transient climate response Why does warming lag behind forcing? CMIP6 plus a two-layer model Figures 7.17 and 7.18b
3. Moisture and rainfall Why do atmospheric moisture and rainfall change at different rates? CMIP6 water-cycle analysis Figure 8.16
4. Arctic sea ice How does sea-ice loss relate to warming and CO₂ emissions? Observations and CMIP6 Figure 9.14
5. Cumulative emissions Why does warming scale with cumulative CO₂? Simple carbon-cycle–climate model Figure 5.31
6. Internal variability Can the same model produce different warming trends? Initial-condition large ensemble Figure 1.13
7. Projection uncertainty Where does uncertainty in future warming come from? Models, scenarios, and ensemble members Figure 1.15
8. Past projections How well did early models predict subsequent warming? Published projections and observations Figure 1.9
9. Land–ocean contrast Why does land warm more than the ocean? CMIP6 warming patterns Figure 4.31
10. Extreme heat Do the hottest days warm at the global-average rate? Regional daily CMIP6 temperatures Figure 11.3
11. Ocean heat content Where is the excess energy stored? Gridded ocean temperatures Figure 9.6a
12. Attribution How do we identify the human contribution to warming? Observations and forcing experiments Figure 3.9

AI assistance and a trustworthy data pipeline

AI assistance is permitted for this project. You may use it to locate documentation, explore methods, generate or debug code, and help process data. Check scientific claims against relevant papers, reports, or dataset documentation. You are responsible for every submitted calculation and explanation, including code developed with AI assistance.

Your notebook should let another student follow the complete chain:

source data or model → selection → processing → calculation → figure → scientific interpretation

For each major stage, explain what enters, what changes, and why. Document the dataset or model version; experiment, scenario, and member selections; variables and units; time periods and baselines; spatial masks and weights; and treatment of missing values, control drift, or uncertainty where relevant.

Useful checks include:

  • Comparing one model’s result against a published value or independently calculated diagnostic.
  • Verifying that an area-weighted average has sensible weights and a sensible magnitude.
  • Checking a unit conversion or a time integral by hand for a small example.
  • Testing a conservation law, limiting case, or known solution in a simple model.
  • Repeating a calculation with a different averaging window or another reasonable analysis choice.

Show the numerical result of each check and explain what it establishes. A plausible-looking plot or a statement that “the code ran” is insufficient evidence.

Include a short AI-use statement identifying the tools used, the tasks they helped with, and how you checked their output. Describe a concrete example of how you verified, corrected, or rejected AI output; if you did not use AI, say so. Cite external code and adapted workflows. Write the scientific interpretation in your own words and ensure every team member can explain it. Being able to run AI-generated code or repeat an AI-generated explanation is not sufficient: you should understand why the method is appropriate, what assumptions it makes, where it could fail, and what the results do and do not establish.

Notebook and presentation

Reproducible notebook

Submit a notebook that tells a scientific story, with explanatory text between code cells. Include:

  • The physical question, relevant scientific references, data sources, and agreed scope. Include a target figure reference if you use one.
  • A concise explanation of the physics and the equations or diagnostics used.
  • Data access or model setup, followed by the documented processing pipeline.
  • Your results, physical interpretation, validation checks, and any comparisons or sensitivity analyses used to support your conclusions.
  • Conclusions, limitations, references, AI-use statement, and a brief account of each member’s contributions.

Provide a README with run instructions and a record of the software environment. Remove private credentials and machine-specific paths. Identify data sources and retrieval steps; explain any access requirements, cached intermediates, or long-running steps. Restart the kernel and run the workflow in order before submission, and retain the final outputs for review. A separate written report is not required.

Presentation

Use your 20-minute presentation to lead the class from the physical question to the evidence. Explain the relevant physics and scientific context, show the essential pipeline and checks, and present the evidence for your conclusions and their limitations. Choose figures and equations that you can explain clearly within the available time.

Every team member should speak and be ready to answer questions about the overall project. We may ask you to explain a processing choice, interpret an unexpected result, discuss an alternative explanation, or predict how the system would respond to a change. Divide the work as useful, while making sure that understanding is shared across the team.

How the project will be evaluated

Personal physical understanding and the ability to communicate it are the central criteria. The notebook and presentation should make that understanding visible; polished plots and working code alone are not enough. The final project will be assessed on:

Criterion What strong work demonstrates
Physical understanding Each student can explain the mechanisms, quantities, assumptions, and scientific context in their own words and respond to follow-up questions.
Analysis and validation Appropriate methods, a traceable pipeline, and checks that support the result.
Interpretation and judgment Reasoning that goes beyond descriptive statistics, weighs alternative explanations, and recognizes uncertainty, nuances, and limitations.
Reproducibility and communication An organized notebook, useful figures, clear presentation, accurate citations, and shared understanding.

Ambition is valuable when it leads to understanding. A well-scoped analysis with convincing checks can be stronger than a much larger analysis that the team cannot explain.

Resources for the suggested IPCC topics

  • IPCC AR6 WGI report and chapters
  • IPCC figure data and code access
  • IPCC WGI figure-code repositories

Published code and processed figure data are useful references and comparison targets. Explain any parts you reuse and carry out the agreed analysis yourself. For projects that reproduce a published result, some archived workflows require adaptation to current software or computing environments; a smaller, transparent reproduction is an appropriate goal.
